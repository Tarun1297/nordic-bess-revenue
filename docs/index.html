1. The question

Where does a battery in Finland earn its money, and how has that changed?

We study one standard battery: — MW of power and — MWh of energy. Think of it as a water tank with a pipe.

2. Why the grid pays batteries

Electricity supply and demand must match every second. The sign of a mismatch is the grid's frequency: it sits at 50 Hz when supply equals demand, drops when there is too little power and rises when there is too much. Fingrid, Finland's grid operator, pays for capacity that reacts within seconds to pull it back. These products are called Frequency Containment Reserves (FCR).

FCR-N

"Normal operation." Keeps frequency between 49.9 and 50.1 Hz. Works in both directions, all day.

FCR-D up

"Disturbance, upward." Adds power when frequency falls below 49.9 Hz. Must hold for 20 minutes.

FCR-D down

"Disturbance, downward." Absorbs power when frequency rises above 50.1 Hz. Must hold for 20 minutes.

3. Two ways to earn

Standing ready

revenue = price (EUR/MW/h) × MW offered × 1 h

Example: 10 EUR/MW/h × 0.5 MW = 5 EUR for that hour.

Trading

revenue = 0.88 MWh × sell price − 1 MWh × buy price

The battery buys 1 MWh over one hour and gets 88% back to sell. Example: buy at 20, sell at 120 EUR/MWh: 0.88 × 120 − 20 = 85.60 EUR.

4. The rule that limits the bid

Fingrid does not let a battery promise its full 1 MW. For every MW it offers, it must keep spare energy and spare power, so it can keep reacting and still manage its charge level. The amounts come from Table 10 of the Nordic FCR Technical Requirements (valid in Finland from 14 May 2025).

Why this matters: if the full 1 MW could be bid, FCR-N would earn — and FCR-D — EUR per MW a year, and FCR-N would look best. Under the real rules the ranking flips.

5. What the battery must do in a long disturbance

FCR-D must last at least 20 minutes at full power. That is a minimum, not a finishing line: the battery keeps reacting for as long as frequency stays off.

Fingrid's requirements say the FCR response "shall remain activated as long as the frequency deviation persists", and that this applies to batteries too (Nordic FCR Technical Requirements, §3.5). The 20 minutes is how much energy the battery must hold so it can almost always last until slower reserves take over.

Why 20 minutes

5 minutes of normal operation (frequency outside 49.9–50.1 Hz for 5 minutes puts the grid in alert state) plus 15 minutes of full output in alert state. It sets how much energy the battery must hold: 20 minutes at its full FCR-D bid, in each direction.

If it runs low: wind down gently

If the event lasts so long that only 5 minutes of full output is left, the battery switches to Alert State Energy Management (AEM). It eases its output to zero over a few minutes instead of cutting out, which would jolt the grid. While in AEM it counts as not available.

Afterwards: refill within 2 hours

Once frequency is back between 49.9 and 50.1 Hz, the battery must restore its full capacity within 120 minutes. That is what the spare 20% of power in Table 10 is for, and why the bid is 0.833 MW rather than 1 MW.

Not covered here: what being unavailable in AEM costs the owner. That is set in Fingrid's market terms, which this project has not used.

6. Five strategies we compare

FCR-N only

Offer the FCR-N bid every hour.

price(FCR-N) × FCR-N bid

FCR-D up + down

Offer both FCR-D products every hour.

(price up + price down) × FCR-D bid

Day-ahead arbitrage

One charge and one discharge a day, charge first. Each leg is one hour: four 15-minute prices since October 2025, one hourly price before.

0.88 × avg sell price − 1 × avg buy price

FCR-D + one trade

FCR-D every hour except one charge hour and one later sell hour. The trade is sized so FCR-D keeps its 20-minute energy reserve each way: — MWh a day. It trades only when that beats the FCR-D income it gives up.

FCR-D day − FCR-D in 2 hours + trade

Daily best-of

Each day, take whichever strategy earned most. Assumes we knew prices in advance: a ceiling for these strategies, not for every schedule (section 7).

7. How the strategies fit into one day

A battery can't sell the same MW twice. It decides, the day before, which hours (or which part of its power) go to reserves and which go to trading.

The markets run in a fixed order. Day-ahead energy prices are known first, so the owner can pick the hours worth trading and offer the rest of the day to FCR. Each FCR price is per hour, so the battery can be in FCR at 14:00 and trading at 18:00.

There are three ways to combine the strategies in one day. This benchmark uses the simplest one.

Why not do both at full size?

A promised MW has to stay available. And a battery that has just charged to full has no room to absorb power for FCR-D down, so trading changes what FCR it can offer in the next hours.

Why FCR-N and trading clash most

FCR-N needs one hour of energy in each direction, so it wants the battery half full all hour. A trade pushes the charge level to one end.

What the benchmark models

Whole-day strategies (schedule A) and schedule B as "FCR-D + one trade". Splitting the power (C) or trading more than once a day is not modelled, so the daily best-of is a ceiling for the modelled strategies, not for every schedule.

8. How every number is made

One command runs every step below and rebuilds every figure from scratch.

1 Extract

Downloads hourly prices for FCR-N (dataset 317), FCR-D up (318) and FCR-D down (283) from Fingrid, and Finnish day-ahead prices from ENTSO-E, one file per month. Months already on disk are skipped.

2 Validate

Checks: timestamps are explicit UTC, no duplicate intervals, every day complete, no gaps, and spikes above the 99.9th percentile flagged. Day-ahead prices switching from hourly to 15-minute steps on 1 Oct 2025 is flagged as expected. A wrong time zone or a duplicate stops the run.

3 Transform

Turns the raw rows into one table, time · product · price · unit, in CET: the market's own clock. FCR prices are hourly; day-ahead prices keep their 15-minute steps.

4 Revenue

FCR: price × bid for every hour, summed by CET market day. Arbitrage: the best one-hour buy block followed by a later one-hour sell block, each day. FCR-D + one trade: FCR-D in all hours but the best charge and sell hour, using hourly day-ahead prices. Then the daily best-of is picked, and everything is summed by month and year.

5 Report

Writes the monthly table, a summary file and the charts. "Annualised" means a period's revenue scaled to 365 days, so part years compare fairly.

10. Limits: what these numbers leave out

  • Perfect foresight. Real bids are placed the day before prices are known. The best-of and the arbitrage figures are ceilings.
  • Only the modelled schedules. Splitting the power between FCR and trading, or trading more than once a day, is not modelled and could earn more (section 7).
  • Full availability. No maintenance or outages.
  • No bidding risk. Every bid is assumed to be accepted at the clearing price.
  • No costs. No battery wear, grid fees or market fees.
  • Capacity payments only. Payment for energy actually delivered during activations is not counted.
  • Hourly FCR market and day-ahead only. The yearly FCR market, aFRR, mFRR and intraday trading are not modelled.
  • Today's rules for every year. The 2025 bid limits are used for 2022–2025 too, so years compare like for like.

11. Glossary

MW (megawatt)
Power: how fast energy flows. The width of the pipe.
MWh (megawatt-hour)
Energy: 1 MW flowing for 1 hour. The size of the tank.
BESS
Battery energy storage system: a grid-connected battery.
Fingrid
Finland's transmission system operator (TSO). It runs the high-voltage grid and buys reserves.
Frequency (Hz)
How fast the grid's AC current alternates. 50 Hz means supply matches demand.
FCR
Frequency Containment Reserve: capacity that reacts automatically within seconds to stop frequency drifting.
FCR-N
The "normal" reserve. Acts continuously, both directions, within 49.9–50.1 Hz.
FCR-D up / down
The "disturbance" reserves. Up discharges below 49.9 Hz, down charges above 50.1 Hz.
Capacity payment
Money for promising to be available, paid whether or not the reserve is used.
Clearing price
The hourly price Fingrid pays every accepted bid, set by the most expensive bid it accepts.
EUR/MW/h
Price per MW promised, per hour. 10 EUR/MW/h on 0.5 MW for one hour pays 5 EUR.
Bid
How many MW the battery offers into a product for an hour.
Limited energy reservoir (LER)
Fingrid's term for a unit with small storage, like a battery. LER units must keep the spare energy and power in Table 10.
Table 10
The table in the Nordic FCR Technical Requirements listing how much spare power and energy each MW of FCR needs.
Alert state
What the grid is in after frequency has been outside 49.9–50.1 Hz for 5 minutes. Batteries may not refill themselves then.
AEM (Alert State Energy Management)
A battery that is nearly empty in a long event eases its FCR output to zero gently instead of cutting out. It then counts as not available.
Refilling (energy management)
After an event, the battery moves its charge back into the safe zone, using its spare 20% of power. Fingrid calls this Normal State Energy Management (NEM).
mFRR
Manual Frequency Restoration Reserve: slower reserves the grid operator activates. They take over from FCR-D within about 12.5 minutes.
Stacking
Selling FCR-D up and FCR-D down from the same battery in the same hour. Allowed, with both reservations added.
Day-ahead market
The auction, run the day before, that sets a price for every 15 minutes (every hour before October 2025) of the next day.
Day-ahead arbitrage
Buying energy in a cheap hour and selling it in a dearer hour of the same day.
ENTSO-E
The association of European grid operators. Its Transparency Platform publishes day-ahead prices.
Round-trip efficiency
Share of energy you get back after charging and discharging. 88% means 12% is lost.
Perfect foresight
Assuming all prices are known in advance. Gives the most the modelled strategies could earn, not a forecast.
Annualised
Revenue scaled to a full 365-day year, so part years compare fairly.
Saturation
When so many batteries offer into a small market that its prices fall.
CET
Central European Time (CEST in summer): the clock the Nordic market days run on.
YTD
Year to date: from 1 January to the latest month with data.

What this means for new Nordic projects

My reading of the results. Each point gives the evidence first, then what I take from it.

The data is Finland's; Sweden, Norway and Denmark share the same FCR rules but have their own prices.

1. Don't build the business case on FCR

—EUR per MW from FCR-D in —, its best quarter
—EUR per MW from FCR-D in —
—FCR-D, last 3 months against the first 3 of the last 12

Reserve income in Finland has fallen fast and has not clearly stopped falling. A project that needs FCR prices to recover is betting against a market that more batteries keep joining.

2. The value is in combining products and switching between them

FCR-D with one daily trade was the best strategy on — of — days, and in — FCR-N overtook FCR-D for the first time. Which product pays best now changes from quarter to quarter. An operator that mixes products within a day, and moves between them as prices shift, earns more than one that commits to a single product: the optimiser matters as much as the battery.

3. Choose the battery's duration for trading, not for reserves

—EUR per MW, daily best-of, 1-hour battery
—EUR per MW, daily best-of, 4-hour battery
—EUR per MWh of storage at 4 hours, against — at 1 hour

None of the gain comes from FCR-D, which is capped by power and stays at —. FCR-N rises until about 1.5 hours of storage, where the power rule takes over, and stays at — from 2 hours on; arbitrage grows with every hour. Per MWh of storage the best-of falls from — at 1 hour to — at 2 hours and — at 4 hours. On these numbers a 2-hour battery is the balanced choice: it gets the largest FCR-N bid the rules allow and far more trading, while losing less per MWh than a 4-hour one. Whether 4 hours pays depends on battery cost per MWh and on trading more than once a day, and this model covers neither. See the duration table.

4. The open questions are the rules and the markets not modelled here

Fingrid's Table 10 halves a 1-hour battery's FCR-N bid: — EUR per MW against — if the full 1 MW could be bid. The Nordic FCR Technical Requirements note that the FCR-N energy requirement is reviewed and may move to 1–1.5 hours, which would change which battery sizes pay. The next step for this benchmark is aFRR, mFRR and intraday trading. I have not modelled them, so I make no claim about their size.
